Teaching Ayah at Home — Pre-K Guide (Age 4)

A practical, play-based plan for teaching a 4-year-old who already knows the alphabet and can count to 10 how to blend letters and read, write, and do early math, in no more than 1 hour a day, split into short bursts so she doesn't get bored.

The short version

  1. Buy three things (about $55 total): Preschool Math at Home (Kate Snow), Handwriting Without Tears: Get Set for School workbook, and Bob Books Set 1. Get a set of magnetic letters and a cookie sheet too.
  2. For phonics, use ABC See, Hear, Do (Level 1, capitals): letters in groups of 4–5 with a picture and hand motion each, and real CVC words right after each group. The plan in this repo follows that grouping (A B C T M first). Level 2 (lowercase) comes next.
  3. Install Khan Academy Kids (free, no ads). It's the best "screen time that's actually learning" for this age.
  4. Daily rhythm: four 10–15 minute blocks with movement in between. Reading, writing, math, then play/read-aloud. Stop each block before she's done, not after.
  5. Follow the 12-week plan. Weeks 1–6 = one letter group a week, blending words from day 4 of week 1. Weeks 6–12 = Bob Books, sentences, heart words, then lowercase.

Golden rules for a 4-year-old

  • Sounds, not names. She knows "bee, see, dee". Reading needs /b/ /k/ /d/. Every letter gets its sound, picture, and hand motion from the book.
  • Short beats long. 10 focused minutes beats 30 distracted ones. If she's wiggling, it's over.
  • Play is the lesson, not the reward. Every skill in this plan has a game version. Use the game version first.
  • Hands before pencil. Playdough, tweezers, chalk and finger-tracing come before worksheets. Pencil grip and letter formation come from strong hands.
  • Read to her every day. 15–20 minutes of you reading picture books is the single most powerful thing in this whole plan. It counts as part of the hour.
  • Never test, only play. If she gets it wrong, just say the right answer cheerfully and move on. No drilling.
  • Take days off. 4–5 days a week is plenty. Progress at this age comes in bursts, not straight lines.

Phonics and blending

Ayah already knows letter names, so the job is: letter names → letter sounds → hearing sounds blend (orally) → blending printed 3-letter words → reading little books. Any program you choose should do those steps in that order. All of these do.

ABC See, Hear, Do — Level 1 (top pick, and what this plan follows)

  • What it is: A ~$15–20 picture book (26 lessons, one per letter) that teaches each capital letter's sound with an animal picture and a hand motion. Letters come in groups of 4–5, not A to Z, and after every group the child sounds out CVC words using only those letters (the first group gets cat and mat). By the end she has read 55 words, and a review page adds 48 more. Level 2 does the same with lowercase; later levels add blends, digraphs, and sight words. There's also a matching activity guide, coloring book, and writing-practice book per level.
  • Pros: Cheap. Multisensory (see, hear, do) so it suits a wiggly 4-year-old. Blending starts in the first week, which keeps her motivated. Simple enough that you don't need a teacher's manual.
  • Cons: It's a book, not a full program: no games, no writing, no math. That's what this repo adds around it. Do one letter a day and the group's words all week; don't rush through the book in a fortnight.
  • Verdict: Use it as the phonics spine. Buy Level 2 (lowercase) around Week 10.

The Good and the Beautiful (TGTB) — Preschool and Kindergarten Prep

  • What it is: A gentle, open-and-go homeschool curriculum. The Preschool course (ages 3–5) covers letter names and sounds, early math, and fine motor skills. The Kindergarten Prep course book (30 lessons) is the bridge to kindergarten: letter sounds, blending, and beginning handwriting. Cathy Duffy's review notes Kindergarten Prep takes "a few months rather than a year", which matches this 3-month plan almost exactly.
  • Pros: Very affordable (the course PDFs are free to download from their site; printed books are around $30–40). Beautiful, calm design. Short lessons. Parents describe it as "not just cute, it's solid, structured, and it works".
  • Cons you should know:
  • It is a faith-based (Christian) curriculum. Stories, songs, and examples include religious content. Some families love that; if it doesn't fit your family, choose Logic of English instead.
  • Kindergarten Prep asks for more pencil control than many 4-year-olds have (Cathy Duffy's review makes the same point). Skip or shrink the writing parts and do handwriting through HWT instead.
  • The Preschool course will feel too easy on letter names for Ayah, since she already knows them. If you go TGTB, start with Kindergarten Prep and go slowly, or just use the Preschool course's letter-sound pages.
  • Verdict: Great choice if the faith content is welcome and you want cheap and simple. Use it as the spine and add the games from this repo.

Logic of English — Foundations A

  • What it is: A secular, research-based (Orton-Gillingham style) program for ages 4–7. Level A teaches letter sounds, phonemic awareness, blending, and handwriting all together, through lots of games and movement.
  • Pros: Genuinely play-based; reviewers say it's "very motivating" for even 3-year-olds. Teaches all the sounds a letter can make from the start (e.g., that "a" says three sounds), which pays off later. Includes handwriting, so you can skip a separate writing program. 90-day money-back guarantee.
  • Cons: Most expensive option (~$95–100 for teacher's manual + workbook, plus $40–60 in manipulatives like phonogram cards and game pieces). Lessons are written for 30–60 minutes; you must split them across days to stay under an hour and keep her attention. It is slower-paced than it needs to be for a child who already knows letters.
  • Verdict: Best all-in-one if you want one purchase that covers reading and writing and you don't want religious content. Do half a lesson a day.

Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons

  • What it is: A $15–20 scripted book, 20 minutes a day, based on Direct Instruction. Very strong research track record.
  • Cons: Dry. Uses a special font with marks that she'll later have to unlearn. The reviews and parent reports agree: it works, but children (especially 3–4-year-olds) often tire of it once the stories get longer. For a child who gets bored easily, this is the risky pick.
  • Verdict: Only if she likes structure and you like a script. Not my first choice for Ayah.

All About Reading — Pre-reading

  • What it is: ~$90 kit with a puppet (Ziggy), letter cards, and activity sheets covering letter names, sounds, and rhyming. Very fun, very gentle.
  • Cons: It does not teach blending; that's Level 1 (another ~$120). Since Ayah already knows letter names, half of Pre-reading is review. Poor value here.
  • Verdict: Skip. You'd outgrow it in a month.

Bob Books (Set 1: Beginning Readers)

  • Twelve small books, each using only a few letters, starting with "Mat sat." Perfect first readers for Month 2–3 of the plan. Set 2 continues the same way. Around $15 per set. Buy Set 1.

Free reading resources worth using

  • Khan Academy Kids (app, free, no ads): letter sounds, blending, and math, wrapped in games. Best free option by far. Use it as a 10-minute "screen block" when you need a break, not as the main lesson.
  • Teach Your Monster to Read (free on a web browser; the app costs a few dollars): a game where a monster learns sounds and blends. Kids love it. Matches Month 1–2 of this plan.
  • Starfall ABCs (free part of Starfall): letter sounds with animations.
  • Progressive Phonics (free printable PDFs): little stories where you read most of the words and she reads the highlighted ones. Nice for Month 3.
  • Your library: ask for "decodable readers" or "phonics readers." Many libraries have Bob Books to borrow.

Math

Preschool Math at Home — Kate Snow (top pick)

  • What it is: A ~$20 book of simple, sequenced games for ages 3–5: counting, comparing, subitizing (seeing "three" without counting), shapes, patterns, and the first ideas of adding and taking away. Uses household items only. Each activity is about 5 minutes.
  • Why it fits Ayah: It's literally "math as playing." Reviewers report their 4-year-olds called each activity a "math game." No worksheets, no writing required, so it doesn't fight with her fine-motor stage. It also matches her level exactly: she counts to 10, and this builds real number understanding on top of that.
  • What comes next: When she turns 5, move to Kindergarten Math with Confidence by the same author (~$40 for instructor guide + workbook). Families report that transition is smooth.

Kindergarten Math with Confidence

  • Excellent, hands-on, 15–20 minute lessons with a review warm-up, an activity, then a short workbook page. Save this for age 5. Starting it at 4 usually means the workbook pages become a fight.

The Good and the Beautiful Math K

  • Fine if you're already all-in on TGTB and want one look and feel. Otherwise Preschool Math at Home is a better fit for this age and for one-hour days.

Free math resources

  • Khan Academy Kids (again).
  • Board games teach more math than worksheets at this age: Count Your Chickens, Hi Ho! Cherry-O, Hoot Owl Hoot, Candy Land (colors, turn-taking), and later Sum Swamp. Dominoes and dice are gold for subitizing.
  • YouTube: Numberblocks (BBC) is the best math show for this age. 5-minute episodes. It's how many 4-year-olds learn number bonds without noticing.

Writing and drawing

Handwriting Without Tears — Get Set for School (Pre-K)

  • What it is: The preschool level of the most widely used handwriting program in US schools. "Crayon only." Teaches pre-writing strokes, then capital letters in a developmental order (starting with the easy straight-line ones: L, F, E, H, T, I), plus numbers. Optional extras: wooden pieces to build letters, the "Mat Man" song, a slate chalkboard for "wet-dry-try."
  • Pros: Multisensory, songs, building letters with pieces before writing them. One reviewer said it was a great fit for a very active 4-year-old because it "held their attention" with lots of ways to practice the same letter. Rated around 4/5 by parents.
  • Cons: The workbook alone is a little dull if you skip the hands-on parts. You don't need the full kit; the workbook (~$15–20) plus homemade wood pieces or playdough is enough.
  • Verdict: Buy the workbook. Do the activities in 04-activity-bank.md before each page.

Drawing

You do not need a drawing curriculum at 4. Drawing is handwriting prep: every circle, line and zigzag she draws builds the hand for letters. What helps:

  • Free drawing daily with chunky crayons, markers, and chalk. Big paper, and vertical surfaces (easel, paper taped to a wall, sidewalk chalk) build shoulder and wrist strength.
  • Art for Kids Hub (YouTube, free): "how to draw" videos with a dad and his kids. Pick the simplest ones.
  • Ed Emberley's Drawing Book of Animals (~$10): shapes-to-animals drawing. She can follow the little steps with you.
  • Process art: painting with q-tips, sponge stamping, cutting with kid scissors, sticker collages. All of these are fine-motor practice.

What to NOT buy yet

  • Any lowercase cursive or extensive lowercase handwriting program. Capitals first at 4.
  • Sight-word flashcard packs. She'll read most "sight words" by sounding out anyway. A handful (the, a, I, is, to, and) is enough for now.
  • Full kindergarten box curricula. She's 4. Three light subjects and a lot of play is the right dose.
  • Tablet apps beyond Khan Kids and Teach Your Monster. Most "learning" apps are candy with a letter on it.

How to Teach a 4-Year-Old: Reading, Writing, Math

This is the "method" file. Read it once before you start; come back to it when something isn't working.

The one-hour day

Attention span at 4 is roughly age + 1 to age × 3 minutes, so 5–12 minutes of real focus per sitting. The hour is therefore four short blocks, never one long one. Between blocks: jump, dance, snack, run outside.

Block Minutes What Feels like
1. Sounds & reading 10–15 The reading step for this week (see plan) A game with letters
2. Move 5 Anything physical. Non-negotiable. Recess
3. Hands & writing 10 Fine-motor play, then 1 HWT page or a letter Craft time
4. Math 10 One Preschool Math at Home game or plan activity A game with snacks/toys
5. Read-aloud 15–20 You read picture books to her. Anytime; bedtime is fine. Cuddle time

Total: 50–60 minutes, and only ~30 of it is "lessons." You can split it across the day: sounds after breakfast, math before lunch, reading at bedtime.

Rules that keep her engaged - Stop early. End every block while she's still having fun. "One more!" from her is the goal; "I'm done" is a signal you went too long. - She picks the order (within reason). Choice is a huge motivator at 4. - Same time, same spot. A predictable "learning basket" on the table after breakfast removes the negotiation. - Praise effort and specifics: "You slid those sounds together all by yourself!" beats "Good job." - Bored = switch, not push. Every skill has 5+ game versions in the activity bank. Change the game, not the goal. - Skip a day guilt-free. Sick, grumpy, big outing? Skip. Read at bedtime and call it done.

Reading: from letter names to reading words

Reading has four steps. Steps 1–3 run together from Week 1 (learn a few sounds, blend them into words, repeat with the next group). Most 4-year-olds who know the alphabet get to step 4 in about 2–3 months at this pace, but some need 6. Both are normal.

Step 1 — Letter sounds (Weeks 1–6, one group a week)

She knows "bee". She needs /b/.

  • Say sounds cleanly, with no "uh" at the end. "mmm" not "muh", "sss" not "suh", "t" as a quick puff not "tuh". This matters enormously for blending later: "muh-a-tuh" never turns into "mat", but "mmm-aaa-t" does.
  • Teach short vowels first (a as in apple, e as in egg, i as in igloo, o as in octopus, u as in umbrella). One sound per letter for now; c = /k/, g = /g/ hard, y = /y/.
  • Teach letters in small groups and blend words right away (the ABC See, Hear, Do approach). Group 1 is A, B, C, T, M; with just those she reads cat, mat, bat, tab, cab, at, am. One new letter a day, then words from that group all week. The full group order is in 03-three-month-plan.md; if your book's groups differ, follow the book.
  • Capitals first, like the book's Level 1 (it also matches handwriting, which starts with capitals). Use capital magnetic letters now; add lowercase in Week 11 by matching each small letter to its capital.
  • Attach each sound to the book's picture and hand motion. Do the motion every time you say the sound; actions lock sounds in memory.
  • 2–3 minutes of review daily: flash the letters she knows, she says the sound. Make it a race, a whisper game, a "trick the puppet" game.

Step 2 — Hearing sounds blend (oral blending, alongside Step 1, no letters)

Before she blends printed letters, she must hear that /k/ /a/ /t/ is cat. This is done with no letters at all, just voices.

  • Robot talk: "Please pass the c-u-p." She figures out "cup!" Do it constantly: at meals, in the car, with toys.
  • Start with 2 sounds ("m-e" = me, "u-p" = up), then 3 ("s-u-n"). Start with continuous sounds (m, s, n, f, l, r, v, z) because they can be stretched: "mmmaaannn."
  • Reverse it (segmenting): "How do we say dog like a robot?" "d-o-g!"
  • First-sound games: "I spy something that starts with /m/." Rhyming: "cat, hat, bat, ___?"

She's ready for step 3 when she can hear "s-u-n" and say "sun" most of the time.

Step 3 — Blending printed words (starts Week 1, after the first letter group)

Now put the letters in front of her.

  • Use 3 magnetic letters on a cookie sheet. Say the sounds slowly while you slide your finger under them: "sssaaat... sat!" Then she does it.
  • Continuous blending is the best first method: hold each sound and glide into the next with no stops. "mmmaaap" → "map."
  • Successive blending if that's hard: blend the first two, then add the third. "sa... sat."
  • Word families: change one letter at a time. sat → mat → map → mop → top → tip. One letter swap per turn keeps it easy and a little magical.
  • CVC words only (consonant-vowel-consonant): cat, sun, pig, dog, bed. No "silent e", no "th/sh", no "ee" yet. That's for later.
  • Use only the letters she has so far. Week 1 words come from A B C T M; each new group adds new words. Short-e (Week 3) is the hardest vowel to hear, so lean on the hand motion.
  • Tiny doses: 3–6 words a session. Five minutes. Done.

Step 4 — Reading little books and sentences (Weeks 6–12)

  • Start Bob Books Set 1 when she can blend most CVC words with a little help. Book 1 is "Mat sat." She'll read a whole book in the first sitting and be very proud.
  • Introduce a few "heart words" (sight words that don't follow the rules yet): the, a, I, is, to, and. Just tell her: "This one we know by heart: the." Two per week max.
  • Point-and-slide: she puts her finger under each word and slides as she blends.
  • Reread. Reading the same tiny book 3 days in a row builds speed and confidence. That's not cheating; that's fluency.
  • You still read to her every day. Her own reading is 5 minutes; your read-aloud is 15–20.

Things that look like problems but aren't

  • Guessing from the picture → cover the picture, point to the first letter, "what sound?"
  • "kuh-a-tuh... cup?" → sounds too choppy. Go back to continuous blending and stretchy words (sun, man, fin).
  • Knows all sounds but can't blend → it's a hearing thing, not a letter thing. Two weeks more of oral blending games (step 2), no letters.
  • Reads a word, then can't read it 10 seconds later → totally normal at 4. Decoding is the skill; memory comes with rereading.
  • Reverses b/d → normal until 6–7. Teach "b has a belly, d has a diaper" or the "bed" trick with fists. Don't worry.

Writing: hands first, letters second

At 4, most of "writing" is building the hand. The pencil is the last step, not the first.

Readiness checklist (work on these whichever aren't there yet)

  • Holds a crayon with fingers, not a fist (a tripod grip starts to emerge at 4; don't force it, but use short crayons and golf pencils, which make a fist grip impossible).
  • Can draw: vertical line, horizontal line, circle, cross (+), then square, diagonal lines, X, triangle. This order is developmental; a triangle usually appears around 5. Capitals need mostly lines and circles, which is why they come first.
  • Can cut with kid scissors along a straight line.
  • Uses tweezers/tongs to pick up small things.

The teaching order

  1. Pre-strokes (weeks 1–3): lines, circles, crosses, in sand, on chalkboards, in the air, on big paper.
  2. Capital letters (weeks 3–12) in the HWT order, because it goes easy → hard: - Frog-jump capitals (start at top left, jump back to top): L, F, E, H, T, I, U, C, O, Q, G, S, J - Then: D, P, B, R, N, M, K, A, V, W, X, Y, Z
  3. Her name in capitals (start whenever she has the letters for it; A-Y-A-H is all easy strokes!).
  4. Numbers 1–10 (Month 3, mixed into math).
  5. Lowercase comes in the next three months, not these.

How to teach a letter

Every letter, same four moves, five minutes total: 1. Build it with wooden pieces, playdough snakes, or popsicle sticks. 2. Trace it big on a chalkboard, in a salt tray, or in the air with a whole arm, while saying the strokes out loud ("big line down, little line across"). 3. "Wet-Dry-Try" (HWT's trick): you write it in chalk, she traces it with a wet sponge, then a dry paper towel, then writes it herself in chalk. 4. One workbook row in crayon. Then stop.

Vertical surfaces (easel, wall paper, fridge, shower wall with bath crayons) strengthen the wrist and set the hand in the right position. Use them a lot.

What to expect

  • Letters will be big, wobbly, and sometimes backwards. Fine.
  • Two or three letters a week is a good pace. Some weeks: none, just playdough. Also fine.
  • Never make her erase and redo. Circle the best one on the row and say why it's the best.

Math: understanding, not just counting

She counts to 10 by rote, which is great. The next layer is knowing what numbers mean. Preschool Math at Home covers all of this in the right order; here's what it's aiming at so you can spot the learning in daily life.

The five big ideas for age 4

  1. One-to-one correspondence: touching one object per count word. "Count the crackers" with real crackers. Slow her down: "touch and count."
  2. Cardinality: the last number you say is how many. "So how many crackers?" If she recounts, she's not there yet. Keep playing.
  3. Subitizing: seeing 1, 2, 3 (and later up to 5) without counting. Dice, dominoes, dot cards, fingers. "How many? Don't count, just look!"
  4. Comparing: more, fewer, same. Two piles of snacks: "Which has more? How do you know?" Line them up to check.
  5. Number sense to 10: knowing 5 is 4 and 1, or 3 and 2 (number bonds). Fingers are the best tool: "Show me 5. Now hide 2. How many are showing?"

Plus the "shape and pattern" strand: naming circle, square, triangle, rectangle; building AB and ABB patterns with blocks, beads, or stickers.

How to teach a math game

  • Real things, always. Snacks, blocks, toy animals, pom-poms in a muffin tin. A worksheet with pictures of apples is a poor substitute for apples.
  • You play too. It's a game, so take turns, sometimes lose, sometimes make a silly mistake for her to catch ("One, two, four!" "Noooo, three!"). Catching your mistakes is a huge learning move.
  • 5 minutes. Then the game "goes back in the basket until tomorrow." Leaving them wanting more is the trick.
  • Narrate math all day: "You have 2 shoes and I have 2 shoes, that's 4 shoes." "We need 3 more plates." "Which cup is taller?" This "math talk" is proven to matter more than any workbook.

Where she should be by the end of 3 months (typical, not required)

  • Counts objects to 10 accurately, rote counts to 20 or so.
  • Recognizes numerals 0–10 (and can match them to quantities).
  • Subitizes to 4 or 5.
  • Compares two groups; knows "more/fewer/same."
  • Adds and takes away within 5 with objects or fingers ("2 cars and 1 more car is 3").
  • Names basic shapes, copies an AB pattern.
  • Writes a few numerals (messy is fine).

Printable version: printables/weekly-plans.pdf has each week below on one page with a Mon–Fri tick grid, ready to hang up. printables/checklists.pdf has the hang-up checklists (letter sounds, words, books, writing, math, sticker chart).

Week 1 is A, B, C, T, M and she reads cat, mat, bat, tab, cab by Friday. No waiting a month for blending. Use the book's page for each letter (picture + hand motion), and use the words printed in the book after each group. If a week goes badly

  • She's resisting: cut everything to 5 minutes for a week, keep only games and story time. Resistance is usually "too long," not "too hard."
  • A skill isn't sticking: repeat the week (use the blank page at the end of the printable). Or go back one step: blending trouble → ear games; letter trouble → hand motions and hunts.
  • She's flying: don't add subjects. More books, more labels, harder games. Depth over speed at 4.

Activity Bank

Short, cheap, play-based activities organized by skill. Numbers are referenced from the weekly plan. Most take 5–10 minutes and use things you already have. Swap freely. If she's bored with one, pick another for the same skill.

Supply basket: lowercase magnetic letters, cookie sheet, index cards, playdough, chalk, salt/sand in a tray, tweezers or tongs, pom-poms, dice, dominoes, sticky notes, a muffin tin, stickers, crayons (broken short), kid scissors, a puppet or stuffed animal.

A. Letter sounds (Month 1)

  1. Sound flash race — flash cards of learned letters; she says the sound. Time it with a silly countdown; try to beat yesterday. 2 minutes.
  2. Trick the puppet — the puppet says the wrong sound ("t says /m/!"). She corrects it, giggling. Kids learn most from catching mistakes.
  3. Sound treasure hunt — hide 5 objects starting with today's sound (sock, spoon, sticker, straw, stone for /s/). She hunts, names each, says the sound.
  4. Letter-sound hopscotch — chalk letters in hopscotch squares. She jumps and says each sound. Add "jump to the letter that says /m/."
  5. Sensory writing — write the letter in a salt tray, shaving cream, or finger paint while saying the sound. Then "erase" and do it again.
  6. Robot dinner — one meal where every request is in robot talk: "pass the c-u-p." Builds oral blending without any letters.
  7. Sound sorting bins — two bins, two letters. She sorts toys by first sound. (Pig, pen, pot → /p/; sock, spoon → /s/.)
  8. I Spy with sounds — "I spy something that starts with /b/." Then she gives you one.
  9. Sound actions — each letter gets an action (look up "Jolly Phonics actions" on YouTube). Play "Simon says /s/."
  10. Alphabet bath — foam letters in the bath; fish out the one that says /t/. Stick it to the wall and say a word.
  11. Rhyme time — "cat, hat, bat, ___?" Then silly nonsense rhymes: "zat, wat!" Rhyming trains the ear for blending.

B. Blending printed words (Month 2–3)

  1. Word family flip book — staple 6 index cards on the left of a card that reads _at; flip the first letter: c, h, m, b, s, r. She reads each flip.
  2. Blending ramp — three letter cards lined up next to a cardboard ramp. Roll a toy car past them as she blends; roll faster each time until the word "pops out."
  3. Cookie-sheet swap — magnets: sat. "Change one letter to make mat." She swaps. Then map, mop, top, tip. One swap per turn.
  4. Secret word treasure hunt — 5 index cards with CVC words that are also hiding places (bed, mat, cup, tub, box). Each word she reads leads to the next clue; the last hides a sticker.
  5. Word building with blocks — write letters on blocks/Duplos; she stacks them to build the word you say ("build sun").
  6. Sentence strips — silly sentences on paper strips: the pig can hop. the cat is wet. She reads and acts it out.
  7. Read to Teddy — she reads each word she built to a stuffed animal, who "claps."
  8. Reading picnic — blanket, snacks, a pile of Bob Books; every stuffed animal gets read one page.
  9. Whisper-shout reading — read the same 5 words in a whisper, then a shout, then a squeaky voice. Rereading = fluency.
  10. Label the house — sticky notes with CVC words on the real objects (bed, cup, pan, mat, box, tub). She reads them as she passes all week.
  11. Story cards about her — write a 2-line story on an index card: Ayah can run. Ayah is 4. She reads it and keeps it in her "my books" envelope.
  12. Word swat — tape 6 words on the wall. Say one; she swats it with a fly swatter and reads it.
  13. Magic reveal — write CVC words in white crayon; she paints over them with watercolor to reveal, then reads.
  14. Progressive Phonics books (free printables) — you read the black words, she reads the red ones.

C. Hands and handwriting

  1. Playdough letters — roll snakes, build today's capital. Then squish and rebuild. Hand strength + letter shape.
  2. Popsicle-stick letters — big sticks (long line) and half sticks (little line) build L, F, E, H, T, I like HWT's wood pieces. Curves: a bent pipe cleaner.
  3. Wet-Dry-Try — small chalkboard. You write the letter in chalk; she traces with a wet sponge, then a dry paper towel, then writes it in chalk herself.
  4. Salt tray strokes — pre-writing strokes (|, —, O, +, X, □) in a tray of salt. Say the stroke: "big line down."
  5. Rainbow letters — one big letter on paper; she traces it with 5 different crayon colors.
  6. Vertical writing — paper taped to the wall, easel, or bath crayons on the tub wall. Builds the wrist and shoulder for good grip.
  7. Tweezers transfer — move pom-poms from a bowl into a muffin tin with tweezers or tongs. Count them as a bonus.
  8. Cutting strips — kid scissors, cut a paper strip into "fringe," then cut along a straight line, then a zigzag.
  9. Sticker lines — put dot stickers along a line, then along a circle, then along a letter's path.
  10. Squeeze and squirt — spray bottle at chalk letters outside; eyedropper into an ice tray; both build finger strength.
  11. Name puzzle — her name on a card, cut into letters; she rebuilds it, then traces it, then writes it.
  12. Draw-and-label — she draws a cat; you write "CAT" lightly; she traces it. Labeling drawings makes writing feel purposeful.
  13. Lacing — cardboard shape with holes and a shoelace.
  14. Air writing — whole arm, huge letters in the air, eyes closed, saying the strokes. Great for wiggly days.
  15. Certificate day — she "signs" her name on a homemade certificate at the end of the 12 weeks.

D. Math

Counting and number sense

  1. Touch and count — line up snacks; she touches each one as she counts. "So how many?" (cardinality). Eat them (subtraction!).
  2. Counting collections — a jar of buttons/rocks/LEGO. Count how many, then "make it 10."
  3. Count the steps — stairs, steps to the mailbox, jumps on the trampoline. Rote counting to 20–30 in real life.
  4. Countdown rocket — 10 fingers, count down to blast-off while crouching then jumping.
  5. Number hunt — find numerals around the house (clock, remote, book pages, mailbox). "Which number is this?"
  6. Muffin tin numbers — number cards 1–10 in muffin cups; she puts that many pom-poms in each (tweezers for bonus fine motor).
  7. Hundred chart pointing — a printed chart; point and count to 20, then 30. Cover a number; "which one is hiding?"

Subitizing (seeing how many without counting)

  1. Dice grab — roll a die, grab that many blocks. "Don't count, just look!" Works for 1–4, then 5–6.
  2. Dot card flash — homemade cards with 1–5 dots in different arrangements; flash for 1 second. "How many?"
  3. Domino match — turn dominoes face up, find two sides with the same number of dots.
  4. Finger flash — you flash fingers behind your back and show them for one second; she says the number. Then she does it to you.
  5. Five-frame — draw 5 boxes; put pom-poms in some. "How many? How many empty spaces?" (number bonds of 5).

Comparing, adding, taking away

  1. More or fewer? — two piles of grapes; "which has more? How do you know? Let's line them up to check."
  2. Hide the pom-poms — 5 pom-poms; cover some with a cup. "How many are hiding?" Number bonds without any symbols.
  3. One more, one less — she has 3 crackers. "I'll give you one more. Now how many?" "The dog ate one. Now?"
  4. Snack math — "You have 2 apple slices and I have 3. How many altogether? Line them up."
  5. Roll and add — two dice, take that many blocks each roll, count the tower.
  6. Toy story problems — "3 cars are in the garage. 2 drive away. How many are left?" with real toy cars.

Shapes, patterns, measurement

  1. Shape hunt — find 3 circles, 3 rectangles in the house. Photograph them.
  2. Popsicle-stick shapes — build a triangle (3 sticks), a square (4). "How many sides?"
  3. Pattern beads — string beads red-blue-red-blue; then red-red-blue. She continues your pattern, then makes one for you.
  4. Body patterns — clap-stomp-clap-stomp; she continues. Then invents one.
  5. Longer or shorter? — line up spoons, forks, crayons. Order 3 things from shortest to longest.
  6. Kitchen scale / cup measuring — pour water into cups: "which holds more?" Bath time counts.

Games that teach math without trying

  1. Board games — Count Your Chickens (cooperative, counting and spinner), Hi Ho! Cherry-O (counting, taking away), Hoot Owl Hoot (colors, cooperative), Candy Land (colors, turn-taking). Play one every day of "board game week" (Week 11).
  2. Numberblocks — 5-minute episodes on YouTube; watch one, then build that number with blocks.
  3. Khan Academy Kids — 10 minutes of the math path when you need a break.

"She's bored" emergency list

Pick one, do it for 5 minutes, and call the block done: - Hopscotch (#4), word swat (#23), air writing (#39), countdown rocket (#44), dice grab (#48), body patterns (#62). - Or: skip the lesson, read two picture books together. That is never wasted time.
